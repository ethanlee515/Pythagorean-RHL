\section{Use of AI in the Research and Formalization}
\label{app:ai-assistance}

This appendix is a historical account of our workflow in 2025--2026.
As of October 2026, we consider it plausible that an agent using GPT-6 Astra,
given this manuscript with its Rocq excerpts (including those in
Appendix~\ref{app:formal-interface-excerpts}) removed and access to Rocq and
the stated dependencies, could independently recreate the full formalization
from scratch for at most US\$200 in model usage.
We have not performed this experiment; both its success and its cost remain
conjectural.

OpenAI Codex was used extensively in both the Rocq development and the
preparation of this manuscript.
Its contributions included implementations, proof scripts, auxiliary lemma
statements, mathematical arguments and counterexamples, explanations of
library interfaces, and drafts and revisions of the exposition.
The authors supplied the research objectives, developed the initial
specifications, directed and reviewed the subsequent work, and retain
responsibility for the definitions, assumptions, results, and text.
The account below draws on the authors' recollection, the Git histories of the
artifact and manuscript, and retained development conversations.
These records support a chronology and concrete examples of collaboration;
they do not support a precise division of credit by line count.
An audit of the records retained on the development machine, performed on
November 4, 2026, also provides the usage and revision measurements below.

\subsection{Human design and early formalization}

The cryptographic objective preceded the intensive AI-assisted proof work.
In 2025, the authors formalized the approximate homomorphic-encryption
interface, correctness properties, and IND-CPA and IND-CPAD security games
largely by hand.
The artifact already contained a statement of the noise-flooding security
reduction in August 2025, although its proof and some quantitative parameters
were still placeholders.
The intended security result therefore guided the later proof development;
the final interfaces, assumptions, and numerical bound were refined during
that development.

The relational logic similarly began with human-written judgments and proposed
rules in LaTeX, followed by manual encodings of the judgments as Rocq
propositions over SSProve semantics.
These early definitions and rule statements were starting points for
verification, and several required revision before soundness could be proved.

\subsection{Joint development of logic soundness}

After a break, substantial AI-assisted work resumed in May 2026.
Much of the initial soundness work proceeded through short, interactive
exchanges: the authors selected a rule or probability lemma, Codex proposed a
proof or decomposed it into auxiliary obligations, and the authors questioned
the statements, discussed the argument, and redirected the next step.
Codex implemented proof scripts and used compiler feedback to revise them.
The collaboration covered the probability results underlying the logic as
well as the program-level rules, including conditional KL composition,
Pinsker's inequality, and discrete-Gaussian analysis.

Two changes illustrate how this process affected the mathematics.
First, in June, discussion of sequencing exposed a hidden-mixture problem:
omitting a shared random intermediate value from the transcript can invalidate
the proposed coordinatewise conditional-KL bounds.
Codex supplied a counterexample during the discussion, and the subsequent
rule design retained such intermediate values as explicit coordinates with
zero KL budget.
These zero entries preserve the conditioning information needed by later
coordinates without increasing the final numerical loss.
Keeping them explicit was part of repairing the sequence rule.

Second, the authors raised the behavior of the judgments on programs with
missing probability mass and asked whether the existing completion
construction could be used for the Pythagorean judgment.
Together with Codex, they developed and proved the completed sequencing rules
and the bridge to additive error.
The resulting definitions account for missing subdistribution mass with an
explicit \(\bot\) outcome, as described in \Cref{sec:pyth-toolkit,sec:artifact}.
Both episodes involved changes to proposed statements and definitions,
followed by proofs of the revised rules.

\subsection{Compiler construction and verification}

The authors identified the need to expose a bounded number of selected oracle
calls in an otherwise arbitrary adaptive adversary, and supplied the intended
algorithmic behavior and semantic preservation requirement.
The trace compiler was developed interactively in early June 2026 from this
design, building on a partial trace/replay implementation that the authors
supplied for review.
Codex filled in substantial parts of the implementation, helped formulate the
correctness theorem, and constructed its proof through intermediate lemmas.
The authors reviewed the construction and corrected proposed restrictions on
the theorem, including an unnecessary bounded-call hypothesis for
same-package semantic correctness: calls remaining after the selected prefix
are handled by the residual linked package.

The discussions also clarified SSProve's operation resolution, raw-code
linking, and package composition interfaces.
The migration to SSProve's nominal extension had already begun in late May,
before the trace compiler, with AI assistance in adapting the constructions
and repairing interfaces.
The quantitative compiler rule was a further joint development: its statement
was revised as the logic evolved, and Codex supplied substantial proofs for
the adaptive replacement theorem and its probability dependencies.
By late June, some of these proof tasks were delegated for extended execution
between author checkpoints, alongside continued interactive review.

\subsection{Application to noise flooding and game-hop exposition}

For the application, the authors supplied the security target, an existing
pen-and-paper proof outline, and the strategy of accumulating per-decryption
KL costs before a single conversion to statistical distance.
From late June through July, Codex carried out substantial implementation and
proof work on the concrete reduction, invariants, local decryption comparison,
and composition of exact and quantitative transitions.
This included extended tasks using Codex's persistent-goal workflow, with
progress records and intervening author discussion of assumptions and proof
strategy.

The detailed game presentations in \Cref{sec:trace-compiler} emerged from
this formal proof and several rounds of manuscript feedback.
Codex drafted the intermediate representations and their explanations; the
authors asked for a conventional cryptographic reduction, explicit endpoints,
and a clear distinction between the original game and its compiled form.
In particular, in July the authors explicitly proposed the seven-stage
\(H_0,\ldots,H_6\) organization, separating the original IND-CPAD experiment,
its linked representation, and its compiled representation before the paid
replacement step.
Later joint revisions distinguished this implementation-level factorization
from the three conceptual games in \Cref{eq:conceptual-game-chain}.
The final presentation therefore reflects both AI drafting and specific human
choices about the proof's structure and explanation.

\subsection{Review, checking, and manuscript preparation}

AI suggestions also introduced errors that required author intervention.
For example, during the application proof an AI-added assumption equating
encodings through different inverse charts was challenged by the authors
because it conflicted with the intended origin-centered charts.
The assumption was removed from the proof route.
The replacement argument compares Gaussian centers in a common chart and then
uses shared postprocessing; its specification and proof were developed through
further discussion and formal verification.
This episode illustrates the separate roles of checking a proof relative to
its assumptions and reviewing whether those assumptions express the intended
cryptographic setting.

Rocq kernel checking and inspection of \texttt{Print Assumptions} were central
to accepting the resulting proofs.
The final trusted base and remaining inherited assumption are documented in
\Cref{sec:tcb}; AI-generated proof scripts are subject to the same checks as
human-written scripts.
Human review concentrated especially on the scheme and game interfaces,
theorem statements, assumptions, and the interpretation of the checked result.

\subsection{Retained records and measured usage}
\label{app:ai-usage-records}

The audit covers records preceding November 4, 2026, 00:00 UTC, thereby
excluding the audit conversation itself.
It identifies 126 user-facing Codex conversations about the artifact and
manuscript, with recorded events from May 29 to October 28, 2026.
Of these, 124 used the artifact's working directory (whose capitalization
changed from \texttt{Mending} to \texttt{mending}); two manuscript conversations
started in its parent directory and were included by explicit identification.
Three associated subagent conversations and six automatic action-review
conversations are counted separately.
Five further conversations about preparing and contributing supporting
MathComp lemmas are reported separately as upstream work.
Presentation slides and other research projects are excluded.
This is an inventory of retained local records, not a complete account of all
AI use: Git records assistance earlier in May, before the available project
conversations begin, and the inspected local Copilot store contains no
conversations identifiable with this project.

Each conversation records cumulative token counters.
We take its last available counter, rather than summing successive cumulative
snapshots or counting repeated context as newly written material.
The 126 conversations report 3,635,028,819 input tokens, of which
3,535,606,912 are cached, and 8,682,819 output tokens, for a reported total of
3,643,711,638 tokens.
Thus uncached input accounts for 99,421,907 tokens; approximately 97.26\% of
recorded input is cached.
The reported 3,078,872 reasoning-output tokens are a component of output,
not an additional term in this total.
These large input counts reflect repeated processing of conversation and
tool context across requests; they do not measure the size of the artifact.

\subsection{Abandoned approaches, cleanup, and human redirection}

Git and the conversations permit some measurement of rework, although they
do not identify a well-defined fraction of ``wasted'' effort.
At artifact revision \texttt{4b8625d}, the history contains 231 commits,
161 with Codex coauthor footers and two with Copilot footers (the categories
may overlap).
At manuscript revision \texttt{e6035b2}, there are 134 reachable commits,
including 114 non-merge commits, of which 77 have Codex footers.
Across the artifact's non-merge history, Git's rename-aware line statistics
report 77,096 insertions and 49,866 deletions in Rocq files under
\texttt{theories/}.
For May--July 2026 alone the corresponding counts are 74,171 and 47,827.
These are cumulative edits, including comments, restatements, simplification,
and repeated editing of the same lines; deleted lines are not necessarily
failed proofs, and coauthor footers do not apportion authorship.

\paragraph{A detour that review did not immediately resolve.}
On May 31 the authors explored sequencing rules whose error budget depended
on a heap location, then on a randomized counter.
At 14:57 UTC the author challenged an AI-added premise that effectively
forced the counter to have a single supported value, and at 15:06 asked to
remove it and revise the interface.
At 17:29 the author again asked whether the approach was sound before
continuing; at 17:35, after noting that the original informal argument did
not go through, the author agreed to further attempts.
The conversation records 89,871,914 total tokens, comprising 976,419 uncached
input, 88,749,568 cached input, and 145,927 output, and approximately
1.71 hours of reported completed-turn duration.
Its API list-price equivalent under the convention above is US\$53.63.
It did not settle the route: on June 3, commit \texttt{078fbd6} replaced the
heap-dependent interface with a fixed tuple of budgets and changed 463 lines
by insertion and 1,160 by deletion across four Rocq files.
This supports an account of an abandoned design with repeated human
questioning, rather than a claim that every token in the conversation was
wasted or that each intervention immediately corrected the course.

\paragraph{Successful corrections to logic statements.}
The June 7 hidden-mixture discussion described above records approximately
4.72 million total tokens and 0.35 hours of reported turn duration.
The author questioned mixture preservation at 02:10 UTC and the soundness of
dropping the zero coordinate at 02:53; commit \texttt{162cc37} records the
repair.
On June 12 at 18:46 UTC the author supplied the missing-mass counterexample:
two programs with null output distributions satisfy a vacuous postcondition,
but have output mass zero, contradicting a proposed full-mass lemma.
That conversation records approximately 33.23 million total tokens and
0.92 hours of reported turn duration.
The June 13 completion conversation records a further 39.59 million tokens
and 1.07 hours, and led to the completed sequencing and conversion rules
in commits \texttt{b81d93b}, \texttt{4242f48}, and \texttt{6a30c03}.
These session totals measure the surrounding discussions and proof work,
not the isolated cost of discovering a counterexample.

\paragraph{Repair of the chart assumption.}
The problematic application route was committed as \texttt{5021745} on
July 9; its title explicitly identifies a false axiom.
In the conversation, the author challenged the common inverse-chart encoding
at 15:27:33 UTC, asked to document the problem before further work at 15:29,
and proposed the shared-postprocessing proof strategy at 15:44.
The preceding part of this application conversation reports approximately
647.73 million total tokens and 26.43 hours of completed-turn duration.
Its API list-price equivalent is US\$424.70.
It includes substantial reusable invariant and compiler work, so this entire
quantity cannot be classified as wasted.
From the objection until the July 10, 04:48:51 UTC completion commit
\texttt{da878fb}, the counter increases by 341,717,678 tokens:
7,348,765 uncached input, 333,597,824 cached input, and 771,089 output.
Completed turns ending in that interval report approximately 12.50 hours;
the elapsed interval is approximately 13.36 hours.
The interval's API list-price equivalent is US\$226.68.
This window also includes other application obligations, author discussion,
and assumption checking, so it measures the observed recovery interval,
not a controlled estimate of the extra cost caused by the false assumption.

\paragraph{Pruning and a limit to pruning.}
On July 13 the author successively requested dependency-based pruning of the
application proof, the program-logic layer, and the probability layer, at
05:25, 06:53, and 07:49 UTC.
Commits \texttt{4dced90}, \texttt{b62edcc}, and \texttt{88465c2} respectively
record 3,933, 1,888, and 1,634 deleted Rocq lines, with 4, 93, and 23
inserted lines: 7,455 deletions and a net reduction of 7,335 lines.
This is evidence of substantial code that was unnecessary for the selected
final dependency path, including alternative wrappers and supporting lemmas.
The conversation reports 88,957,338 total tokens
(1,737,123 uncached input, 86,993,920 cached input, and 226,295 output)
and approximately 3.87 hours of reported completed-turn duration.
Its API list-price equivalent is US\$58.97.
The same review also checked the cleanup criterion: at 08:26 the author
asked to retain the Gaussian tail bound and its dependencies for future
imperfect-correctness work, despite their being unused by the current final
theorem.
The probability cleanup commit retains this result with an explanatory
comment.
Thus dependency pruning required a human decision about future scientific
use, and ``unused by the current theorem'' was not treated as synonymous
with ``worthless.''

\paragraph{Reproducibility and limits.}
The manuscript source includes the extraction script
\path{scripts/audit-ai-assistance.py}, the repricing script
\path{scripts/price-ai-assistance.py}, a data export in
\path{ai-assistance-data/}, and provenance and interpretation notes in
\path{AI-ASSISTANCE-NOTES.md}.
The export records session identifiers, source-log hashes, timestamps, model
identifiers, counters, completed turns, goal updates, Git revisions and line
statistics, and the boundaries of the selected episodes, without exporting
complete private conversations.
Raw conversations remain necessary to reassess the qualitative coding of
human interventions.
Uncommitted experiments, deleted or unavailable conversations, work on other
machines, and unrecorded human reading and thinking remain unmeasured.
The records establish concrete revisions and recovery episodes, but do not
support a causal token budget for mistakes, an overall failure rate, or a
counterfactual comparison with an entirely human development.
