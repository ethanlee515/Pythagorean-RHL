\section{Use of AI in the Research and Formalization}
\label{app:ai-assistance}

Our formalization is heavily AI-assisted.
We describe the development through the human work involved in obtaining the
checked result.
The authors supplied the research objectives and initial specifications,
reviewed definitions and theorem statements, discussed mathematical arguments,
and redirected both proof development and exposition.
OpenAI Codex contributed substantial implementations and proof scripts,
auxiliary lemma statements, mathematical arguments and counterexamples,
explanations of library interfaces, and manuscript drafts and revisions.
The authors retain responsibility for the definitions, assumptions, results,
and text.

This is a historical account of work in 2025--2026, especially summer 2026.
We expect improving agents to change the amount of human guidance and model
usage needed for such a development; we have not tested an independent
reconstruction from the finished manuscript.
We organize the account by the probability analysis, logic, compiler, and
cryptographic application, proceeding chronologically within each part.
The parts were developed together, so their timelines overlap.

The account draws on the authors' recollection, Git histories, and retained
local conversations.
The conversations available for this project begin on May 29, 2026, although
Git records AI assistance earlier in May.
For selected episodes we report both human-request records and completed
agent turns: request records include retries and routine instructions, while
agent turns can also follow automatic continuations of a persistent goal.
These counts describe interaction, rather than distinct mathematical
interventions or hours of human labor.
Dates of conversation events below are in UTC.

\subsection{Real and probability analysis}

The main probability targets were Pythagorean probability preservation for
MathComp distributions (\Cref{lem:verified-pythagorean-preservation}) and the
KL divergence between discrete Gaussians (\Cref{lem:dg-kl}).
The Gaussian distributions had been developed before the intensive
AI-assisted work; an AI-assisted version of their KL calculation entered the
artifact on May 6, 2026.
Work on Pythagorean preservation began on May 16 and continued through the
completion of the Pinsker and compiler-rule analysis on June 21.
The missing early conversations prevent us from giving a total interaction
count for either development.

The main obstacle in the preservation proof was making the countable-sum
argument precise.
The formalization needed both a bound on the iterated KL divergence and
proofs that the relevant series were summable.
On June 18--19, the authors questioned what MathComp's totalized sum meant
when a KL series did not converge, and asked to make finiteness explicit in
the distribution predicate and supporting lemmas.
This changed interfaces used throughout the logic and exposed further proof
obligations.
Codex developed the summability, chain-rule, and data-processing arguments,
while the authors repeatedly asked how the intermediate statements followed
from the intended mathematics and whether their assumptions were necessary.

The authors also used informal proofs as review checkpoints.
On June 19 they asked Codex to write the summability and iterated-chain-bound
arguments in LaTeX before continuing the Rocq proofs.
On June 21 they made the same request for Pinsker's inequality, discussed the
binary-event data-processing route, and asked for a top-down organization in
which each outstanding obligation had an identifiable role in the theorem.
The five retained conversations in this final June 19--21 push contain
60 human-request records and 82 completed agent turns, including work on
compiler dependencies.
They cover roughly three calendar days, rather than three days of continuous
human attention.
By June 20 some obligations were being delegated for extended execution
between author checkpoints.
The records show what the authors reviewed and requested, but do not tell
us whether each checkpoint saved time relative to leaving the agent alone.

On June 22 the authors asked to restore the discrete-Gaussian sampling rules
after the logic interfaces had changed.
That conversation contains eight requests and eight completed turns.
The discussion revisited finite KL and injective pushforwards, illustrating
that an earlier value-level calculation did not by itself discharge every
convergence obligation needed by the later interface.
The subsequent proof work reconnected the Gaussian analysis to the revised
logic.

\subsection{Program logic soundness}

Human design of the logic was underway by July 2025.
The authors first wrote judgments and proposed rules in LaTeX, then encoded
them in Rocq; the earliest retained encodings are dated July 12--14, 2025,
with inference-rule and soundness statements on July 14.
These records do not establish the date of the preceding LaTeX design.
These were prototype definitions and statements, rather than a completed
soundness development.

After a break, intensive AI-assisted development resumed the following year,
in May 2026.
Git records Codex-assisted revisions of the program judgments on May 17,
followed by rule development and a sequencing-soundness repair on May 18.
This work was already underway before the first retained conversations on
May 29, 2026.
All remaining dates in this subsection refer to 2026.
The authors typically selected a rule, asked Codex to prove it or expose
smaller obligations, and reviewed the resulting statements and arguments.
Codex implemented the proofs and revised them using compiler feedback.

Some human review led through unsuccessful designs.
On May 31 the authors explored sequencing with heap-dependent error budgets
and then a randomized counter.
They challenged a premise that effectively forced the counter to have a
single supported value, requested its removal, and later reopened the
soundness question.
The discussion nevertheless continued along this route; on June 3 the
interface was replaced by a fixed tuple of budgets.
Human questioning did not always resolve a detour immediately.

Two later repairs affected the judgments used in the final development.
On June 7 the authors asked whether the proposed Pythagorean predicate
respected mixtures.
Codex supplied a counterexample: hiding a shared random intermediate value
can invalidate coordinatewise conditional-KL bounds.
The repair retained that value as an explicit transcript coordinate with
zero KL budget.
The authors also questioned a subsequent suggestion to drop the leading zero
even when the two initial programs were identical and perfectly coupled.
Keeping the coordinate preserves conditioning information without increasing
the numerical loss.

The June 9--11 sequencing-proof conversation contains 51 human-request
records and 50 completed turns, reaching an initial proved sequence rule on
June 11.
On June 12, however, the authors identified a separate problem with missing
probability mass: two null output distributions satisfy a vacuous
postcondition but cannot justify a claimed full-mass conclusion.
On June 13 they proposed reusing the existing completion construction for
the Pythagorean judgment.
The June 12 review and June 13 completion conversations together contain
28 requests and 28 completed turns.
The revised judgment accounts for missing mass with an explicit \(\bot\)
outcome, and Codex helped develop and prove the completed sequencing and
KL-to-additive-error rules.
The earlier sequencing milestone was therefore followed by a change to the
semantics and further proofs.

The authors also directed cleanup after the application was proved.
On July 13, dependency-based pruning removed 1,888 Rocq lines from the logic
layer and 1,634 from the probability layer, alongside small additions.
The authors explicitly retained the Gaussian tail bound and its dependencies
for future imperfect-correctness work, despite their being unused by the
current theorem.
Deciding what to preserve required a judgment about future scientific use.

\subsection{Compiler construction and verification}

The compiler addressed a problem identified by the authors: the logic could
compose a fixed schedule of oracle calls, whereas a cryptographic adversary
chooses its calls adaptively.
On May 31 an author asked about compiling a general adversary into a factored
form suitable for the logic.
The authors supplied the intended suspension/resumption behavior and semantic
preservation requirement, and brought a partial trace/replay implementation
for review on June 3.
That scaffold had itself already received AI assistance.
Codex filled in substantial implementation details, helped state correctness,
and constructed proofs through intermediate lemmas.

The human work included both interface decisions and corrections to the
proposed theorem.
On June 4 the authors rejected an unnecessary bounded-call hypothesis for
same-package semantic correctness: calls after the selected prefix remain in
the residual program and are handled by linking to the original package.
On June 6 they asked to rework the construction so that the selected external
call and its recorded result appeared at the intended point between
suspension and resumption; the correctness proof was then adapted.
The twelve retained conversations about construction and correctness from
June 3--6 contain 47 human-request records and 45 completed agent turns.
This count includes syntax and library-interface questions, and ends before
the later quantitative replacement proof.

That quantitative rule was developed alongside the logic and probability
analysis through June 21.
The discussions revised its invariant requirements and the treatment of local
computation between selected calls.
The authors continued to review the statement and proof structure, while
Codex supplied substantial proofs, including extended work discharging
assumptions.
The nominal-extension migration and associated SSProve interface repairs had
already begun in late May, before the trace compiler.

\subsection{Application to noise flooding}

For the CKKS-inspired application, the formal target is noise flooding for
the abstract approximate-HE interface specified in \Cref{sec:assumptions};
a concrete CKKS instantiation remains outside the checked result.
The authors had formalized the encryption interface and IND-CPA and IND-CPAD
games largely by hand in 2025.
A security-reduction statement existed by August 11, although its proof and
some quantitative parameters were placeholders.
The authors supplied this target, an existing pen-and-paper outline, and the
strategy of accumulating per-decryption KL costs before one conversion to
statistical distance.

Beginning on June 30, Codex undertook extended proof tasks on the reduction,
invariants, local decryption comparison, and composition of exact and
quantitative transitions.
Across three retained application conversations, up to the first checked
completion milestone on July 10, there are 46 human-request records,
269 automatic goal-continuation records, and 297 completed agent turns.
These are different event counts; the automatic continuations were not
additional human instructions.
The authors reviewed progress records and intervened on assumptions and
proof strategy between the longer runs.

The chart assumptions required a substantial correction.
On July 9 an author challenged an AI-added assumption equating encodings
through different inverse charts, because it contradicted the intended
origin-centered charts.
The author asked to document the problem before proceeding and proposed
comparing the Gaussian samples first, then applying shared postprocessing.
The replacement proof compares centers in a common chart and uses that
postprocessing argument, with the required chart laws made explicit.
Codex implemented the revised route and its proofs.

Review also continued after the July 10 milestone.
On July 11 an author challenged an IND-CPA security bound that was constant
across all adversaries, including computationally unbounded ones.
The interface was revised to let the bound depend on the adversary, and the
final theorem exposes its application to the constructed reduction.
Both this correction and the chart episode show the human task of deciding
whether a formally checked implication has assumptions and a conclusion that
express the intended cryptographic claim.

The authors also shaped the game presentation.
Codex drafted intermediate games and explanations; the authors requested an
explicit reduction and conventional cryptographic endpoints, and on July 10
proposed the seven-stage \(H_0,\ldots,H_6\) organization separating the original,
linked, and compiled experiments.
Later revisions distinguished those implementation stages from the three
conceptual games in \Cref{eq:conceptual-game-chain}.
Codex also contributed extensive drafting and revision elsewhere in the
manuscript, under repeated author review.

Across all layers, acceptance of proofs relied on Rocq kernel checking and
inspection of \texttt{Print Assumptions}.
Human review concentrated especially on interfaces, theorem statements,
assumptions, and the interpretation of the result.
The trusted base and remaining inherited assumption are documented in
\Cref{sec:tcb}.
The July 13 pruning also removed 3,933 application-proof lines; together with
the logic and probability cleanup, this amounted to 7,455 deletions and a net
reduction of 7,335 lines.
Those edits record cleanup of alternatives and unused support code, rather
than a measured fraction of wasted AI work.

We use final cumulative conversation counters, preserving downward
corrections, and count reasoning tokens within output.
Agent hours sum client-reported completed-turn durations, including tools and
waiting within turns; they do not measure researcher labor or pure model
computation.
The formalization category includes proof design, failed routes, supporting
analysis, refactoring, dependency upgrades, builds, and artifact documentation.
The paper category includes exposition, review, figures, excerpts, LaTeX
tooling, and the audit; other work principally concerns the MathComp Analysis
contribution.
Mixed conversations are split at reviewed changes of task purpose.

The authors used monthly OpenAI subscriptions and changed tiers during
development; project-specific subscription expenditure has not been
reconstructed.
The monetary column instead reprices retained usage at the Standard API
rates recorded on November 4, using the observed model identifiers,
ordinary and cached input separately, and applicable long-context premiums.
It gives approximately US\$2,232 for formalization and US\$2,477 for the full
sample, assuming the API attains the recorded cache-read pattern.
The six automatic-review conversations contribute 911,508 tokens to the
table but remain unpriced because their recorded model has no identified
public rate.
Hosted-tool fees, local compute, taxes, and unavailable usage are excluded.
The authors intended this comparison as an illustrative overestimate of the
cost attributable to their subscription workflow; it is not a billing audit
or a strict upper bound on expenditure.
