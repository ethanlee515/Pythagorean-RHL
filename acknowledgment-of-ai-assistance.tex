\section{Use of AI in the Research and Formalization}
\label{app:ai-assistance}

Both our Rocq formalization and paper writing are heavily AI-assisted.
In this appendix, we recount our proof engineering efforts.
We remark that our experience reflects the AI agents of 2025--2026,
especially through summer 2026.
We expect that a state-of-the-art agent would be able to produce Rocq formalization more independently and cheaply.

We organize our account by first by project organization, then proceeding chronologically within each part.
Some parts were developed together, so their timelines overlap.

\subsection{Real and probability analysis}

The main probability targets were Pythagorean probability preservation for
MathComp distributions (\Cref{lem:verified-pythagorean-preservation}) and the
KL divergence between discrete Gaussians (\Cref{lem:dg-kl}).
Work on Pythagorean preservation began on mid-May and continued through mid-June.

The main obstacle in the formalization was convergence in the real analysis sense.
In particular, as KL divergence involves summation over the (possibly infinite) support of the distributions,
a priori, the resulting value may not converge.
This process involves several dozens of interactions with the agent,
where we initially asked the agents to independently dispatch any real analytic convergence obligations.
Whenever an agent takes a long time,
we asked the agent to first write down the argument in LaTeX and reviewed the informal proof strategy ourselves.
It however remains unclear if that provided any significant benefits.

\subsection{Program logic soundness}

Our work on the program logic started sometime in 2025,
where we handwrote some judgments and rules in LaTeX then encoded them in Rocq.
These resulted in prototypes which eventually needed adjustments before their soundness could be established.
After a break, intensive AI-assisted development resumed in May 2026.
In each round of the interaction, we typically selected a rule,
and asked an agent to prove its soundness or break the argument down into lemmas.
We then reviewed the resulting statements and arguments.

Through the formalization process, we uncovered and fixed several soundness bugs and design issues.
For instance, an earlier version of our Pythagorean logic would allow the error budget to depend on runtime values (i.e. the heap memory).
This however introduced an unintended constraint where the judgment becomes too restrictive.
After discussions with the agent, eventually it was decided that the error budgets would be fixed statically.

Another design issue involves the sequence rule, where we initially did not insert zero entries in the vectors.
Though this is not unsound in and of itself, it would make the soundness proofs much harder,
and make the Pythagorean judgment more general than intended.

Finally, another issue arised with subnormalized distributions.
MathComp distributions are subnormalized by default;
this is also a conventional way to treat programs that do not terminate.
We noticed that several of our rules turned out unsound in the presense of subnormalized distributions,
and had our agent add the necessary constraints accordingly.

Finally, we also directed the agents to clean up the formalization.
This trimmed more than a thousand lines from the codebase.
Altogether, this effort took dozens of messages with the agents as well.

\subsection{Compiler construction and verification}

We now discuss the development of the compiler from \Cref{sec:trace-compiler}.
In early June, we produced a partial implementation, and had an agent fill in the details.
The agent produced a correct implementation, though the code quality had to be improved.

The compiler correctness verification was surprisingly uneventful.
We started with relatively little initial human input.
The agent came back with an unnecessarily restrictive theorem statement,
which was then promptly repaired accordingly.
Altogether, the compiler construction and correctness have taken around 45 messages over around a week.

The quantitative compile rule \Cref{thm:compile-replace} required more interaction.
The design of the rule itself, especially its treatment of invariants and memory restrictions, had to be handled carefully.
The formalized rule was a result of many rounds of discussions and iterations with AI agents.
The work spanned roughly two weeks over around 60 prompts to the agent.

\subsection{Application to noise flooding}

For the noise flooding proof, the goalpost was set mostly by hand in 2025.
That is, the security games and noise-flooding transforms had been mostly in place.
By then, we also had written a rough outline of the proof.

After developing the program logic and compiler above,
we tried having an agent independently verify the final security theorem using them.
The process went surprisingly smoothly, though not without human interventions.
For example, the agent added an assumption to our metric charts that in fact does not hold dunder the CKKS setting.
The assumption was removed after discussions and clarifications to the agent.
Moreover, the agent also attempted to state the bound in a statistically secure language;
that is, assuming no adversaries (efficient or otherwise) can break the underlying FHE scheme.
This has also been corrected.
Finally, after the formalization, a clean-up round removed almost 4000 lines of Rocq in this part of the project.

Altogether, this part of the formalization took 46 prompts over 10 days.
