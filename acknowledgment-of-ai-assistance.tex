\section{Use of AI in the Research and Formalization}
\label{app:ai-assistance}

Both our Rocq formalization and the writing of this paper were heavily AI-assisted.
In this appendix, we recount our proof engineering efforts.
Our experience reflects the agents available during development,
especially in summer 2026.
We expect newer agents to produce Rocq formalizations with less human guidance and at lower cost.

We organize our account by part of the project, proceeding chronologically within each part.
Some parts were developed together, so their timelines overlap.

\subsection{Real and probability analysis}

The main probability targets were Pythagorean probability preservation for
MathComp distributions (\Cref{lem:verified-pythagorean-preservation}) and the
KL divergence between discrete Gaussians (\Cref{lem:dg-kl}).
Work on Pythagorean preservation began in mid-May 2026 and continued through late June.

The main obstacle in the formalization was proving convergence of infinite sums.
In particular, the series defining KL divergence need not converge to a finite value without further hypotheses.
We had to make convergence requirements explicit and prove the necessary summability facts.
The final stage involved dozens of interactions with the agents.
We initially asked them to discharge the real-analysis obligations independently.
When progress was slow, we asked an agent to first write down the argument in LaTeX and reviewed the informal proof strategy ourselves.
It however remains unclear if that provided any significant benefits.

\subsection{Program logic soundness}

Our work on the program logic began in 2025,
when we wrote some judgments and rules by hand in LaTeX and then encoded them in Rocq.
The resulting prototypes needed adjustments before their soundness could be established.
After a break, intensive AI-assisted development resumed in May 2026.
In each round of interaction, we typically selected a rule
and asked an agent to prove its soundness or break the argument down into lemmas.
We then reviewed the resulting statements and arguments.

During formalization, we uncovered and fixed several soundness bugs and design issues.
For instance, an earlier version of our Pythagorean logic allowed the error budget to depend on runtime values in the heap.
This led to unintended restrictions in the sequencing rules.
After discussions with the agents, we switched to statically fixed error budgets.
Another issue arose in the sequencing rules, where we initially omitted various zero-cost coordinates.
As a result, we were unable to verify the rules' soundness until they were revised.
% This hid conditioning information needed for the KL bounds and led to an unsound proposed rule.
% We revised the rules to retain these coordinates.
We also encountered an issue with subnormalized distributions.
MathComp's distribution type allows total mass below one, which SSProve uses to represent assertion failure and non-termininating programs.
Several proposed rules incorrectly relied on distributions having full mass.
We worked with the agents to introduce completion of subdistributions and add the necessary side conditions.

Finally, we directed the agents to clean up the formalization,
trimming more than a thousand lines of Rocq script.

\subsection{Compiler construction and verification}

We now discuss the development of the compiler from \Cref{sec:trace-compiler}.
In early June, we discussed the possibilities of such a compiler with an agent.
We then developed a partial implementation, and asked the agent to fill in the details.
We subsequently revised the implementation and improved the code quality.

The initial compiler correctness proof went relatively smoothly.
We initially supplied little proof guidance.
The agent proposed an unnecessarily restrictive theorem statement,
where we then removed a bound on the number of oracle calls.
This involved 47 human prompts in under a week.

The quantitative compilation rule in \Cref{thm:compile-replace} required more interaction.
The design of the rule itself, especially its treatment of invariants and memory restrictions, had to be handled carefully.
The formalized rule resulted from many rounds of discussion and revision with AI agents.
This included about 60 prompts over roughly two more weeks.

\subsection{Application to noise flooding}

For the noise-flooding proof, we had set the main targets largely by hand in 2025.
The security games and a preliminary noise-flooding transform were already in place.
At this point, we also had a rough proof outline in LaTeX.
%The checked result concerns the abstract approximate-HE interface in \Cref{sec:assumptions}; a concrete CKKS instantiation remains outside its scope.

After developing the program logic and compiler above,
we tried having an agent independently verify the final security theorem using them.
The process went surprisingly smoothly, though not without human intervention.
For example, the agent added an assumption that would not hold under the CKKS setting.
We removed the assumption and revised the proof after further discussion.

Reaching an initial proof on July 10 took 46 recorded human prompts over about 10 days.
Afterwards, we identified and fixed another bug:
the theorem was stated in a statistically secure way.
That is, it assumed that no adversaries (efficient or otherwise) can break the underlying FHE scheme.

Finally, a cleanup round removed almost 4,000 lines of Rocq code from this part of the project.
